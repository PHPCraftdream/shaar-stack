Honest limits of the legal basis: the public-peers list states no formal terms of use
and no uptime guarantee; its purpose is that anyone may peer with the listed nodes
and its README advises two or three peers. The DHT bootstrap hosts publish no terms
either; they are documented default entry points. The two bootstrap hostnames are
resolved once by the harness (the product accepts only numeric IPv4); every address
must be globally routable or the run aborts before any contact. Beyond the bootstrap
hosts, joining the DHT means exchanging UDP packets with arbitrary volunteer nodes and
asking about 8 of them to store one signed record of at most 1000 bytes; this is
inherent in the protocol and is bounded only by the byte caps below. The record
contains the runner's own interface addresses (the products do not filter private
ones), which are ephemeral.

### Enforced budgets (per dispatch: one run, zero retries, two runners)

All numbers are the contract file's; the harness also embeds ceilings, so editing the
JSON can only tighten them. They restate the 2026-11-04 conditional bounds.

| Budget | Value | Enforcement |
|---|---|---|
| Runs | 1 per dispatch, Linux only; rerun refused (`GITHUB_RUN_ATTEMPT` must be 1) | Inline check in the plan job and in the harness |
| Product window | at most 120 s per process: Server T0 to T0+105 plus 15 s stop grace, Client T0+20 to at most T0+92 plus grace | Absolute epoch deadlines from one shared origin; SIGINT then kill of the owned PID |
| Job | 15 min step limit, 18 min job limit (the first minutes are an idle wait for the shared origin) | GitHub `timeout-minutes` |
| Yggdrasil TCP connections | at most 2 per job (4 per run) | Metered per socket; a third connection is a violation |
| Relay TCP connections | at most 2 (Server only; only when the contract pins a relay) | Metered per socket; without a relay the row stays zero and is not asserted |
| Bytes per job | NIC rx+tx at most 5 MiB (10 MiB per run); Yggdrasil TCP 3 MiB; relay TCP 256 KiB (zero without a relay); DHT/UDP (NIC minus TCP) 1.5 MiB | Metered every 500 ms; a breach kills the product |
| TCP peers | only the resolved addresses of the allowed hosts | Any other non-loopback TCP peer is a violation |
| APP payload | one exchange, 128 B request and 128 B reply (64 KiB ceiling) | The fixture accepts exactly one connection and one exact request |

Metering uses `/proc/net/dev` (all non-loopback interfaces) and `ss -tine` for sockets
owned by the product PID (inode match). It needs no privilege. It is conservative:
counters are runner-wide, so background traffic counts against the caps and the meter
can over-count but not under-count; sampling is every 500 ms, so an overshoot is at
most one interval; very short failed connection attempts can be missed (the cap on
established connections is what is enforced). UDP bytes are derived, not read per
socket. If `ss` or the counters cannot be read the run is refused (fail closed). A
violation, a meter failure or a crash is a fail: no automatic rerun.

Result of the live check after each leaf goes into the record below.

**Measurement of the computer's NAT (2026-11-17, before FIX-4; scratch scripts outside the repository).** From one fixed local UDP port, KRPC `find_node` and `ping` datagrams went to the public bootstrap host `dht.libtorrent.org` and to up to three nodes it returned (about ten datagrams in all, each node's `ip` field names the address it saw; ports only were printed). Result: one local port was seen as three different external ports by three different DHT nodes (two runs, five distinct external ports for five local ports, none equal to its local port), and the same local port kept the same external port toward the same host across two sockets. So the NAT in front of this computer maps by destination (symmetric) and does not keep ports. ### Leaf NAT-2: bounded port scan for the direct path (owner decision of 2026-11-18)

Advances north-star step 4 (the client tries direct QUIC after a coordinated punch). Evidence: attempt 10 (phone: destination-independent but port-rewriting mapping; computer: destination-dependent).

**Acceptance (observable).** (1) With a fake dialer that completes a handshake only for one address within the ring window around an answered public candidate, `scan_then_connect` dials that address in the attempt whose ring covers it, and probes at most `SCAN_PORTS_PER_ATTEMPT` scan addresses in that attempt, each bounded by `SCAN_SLICE`. (2) No scan, and no behaviour change, for a private, loopback, link-local or shared-address (100.64/10) candidate, for rings after `SCAN_RINGS`, and when the candidate is not public. (3) The trace line `shaar: client punch scan ring=<R> probed=<N> found=<yes|no>` carries counts only. (4) The live attempt records the observed result either way: a direct path with the exact payload over the Native route, or the scan counts with `connected=no`; a result that rules the scan out (for example no ring ever answers because the phone's filtering is port-restricted) is a proven limit for this pair, with Yggdrasil standby as the delivered route.

**Non-goals.** No raw UDP protocol and no new wire format (the Answer's candidate list keeps its four-entry cap and codec); no scan from the Server's side; no port prediction beyond the ring window; no scan of private or shared addresses; no helper server, no libp2p relay, no TUN, root or router change; no change to the ordinary dial of a candidate.

**Mechanism.** The Server's Answer also carries the port the DHT nodes saw for its Mainline socket (the confirmed or single-source observation), next to the existing guess (reflexive IP with the local QUIC port). The Client treats each answered public candidate as a ring centre. In its `r`-th attempt that received an Answer (`r` in `1..=SCAN_RINGS`; counting from the first answered attempt, not from epoch 1, because an attempt without an Answer must not use up the nearest ring) it probes the centre and the ports at distance `(r-1)*SCAN_RING_WIDTH+1 ..= r*SCAN_RING_WIDTH` on both sides, using the slotless `ProbeHandle` (QUIC handshake only, closed at once; no admission slot, so no `Limits` conflict), each probe bounded by `SCAN_SLICE`; the first probe whose handshake completes names the address and the ordinary `connect` dials it (a completed probe proves that the peer answered at some address it knows, not at the probed one: see the open attribution defect recorded under attempt 12). The Server already probes the Client's candidate from its own QUIC socket after each Offer (one burst per epoch, five seconds apart at most), which opens the phone's NAT filter for the Client's IP; a filter that admits only the exact Client port (port-restricted) cannot admit the Client's fresh port, and the scan then cannot succeed.

**Constants and budget.** `SCAN_RING_WIDTH` 24 ports per side, `SCAN_RINGS` 10 (distance up to 240), `SCAN_SLICE` 300 ms (below one QUIC initial retransmission, so one initial packet of about 1.2 KB per probe), so one attempt sends at most 48 probes (about 58 KB) and a whole cycle about 480 probes (about 0.6 MiB of UDP), within the NIC bound of 5 MiB per attempt of the live run. After the tenth attempt the Client scans no more.

**Known cost of the design.** The observed DHT port is a sample of the same carrier port block as the QUIC socket's mapping, not that mapping; whether the two are within 240 ports of each other is exactly what the live attempt measures.

### Leaf NAT-3: measure the phone's NAT before more scanning (owner decision of 2026-11-18, design only)

Advances north-star step 4 (the coordinated punch) of the two-device case. Evidence: attempts 13 and 16 (the clean ones): the record opens, the payload crosses over standby, the direct attempts to the record's candidates and eight scan rings (49 ports each, around the port the DHT nodes saw for the phone's Mainline socket) found nothing; the computer's mapping depends on the destination, the phone's does not and it rewrites the port.

**What is unknown, and why each answer decides the next step.**
1. *The phone's filtering.* If the phone accepts a datagram from any address on a mapped port, only the QUIC socket's external port is missing and a scan can find it. If it accepts only from an address it has sent to, the Client's packet must come from an address the phone has contacted (the computer's IP), which the Server's probes already create. If it accepts only from an address and port it has sent to, the computer's destination-dependent mapping makes the direct path impossible without a relay: that is the physical limit the owner's rule asks to measure before it is recorded.
2. *The offset of the phone's QUIC external port from its DHT port* (the carrier gives a port block; whether the allocation is sequential, random or block-wise decides the shape and the width of a scan, and whether the ring order should be one-sided).

**Measuring step (NAT-3a, a diagnostic and not a product behaviour).** A small command (a `diag nat-ports` subcommand or a harness-only tool, decided at acceptance) that, on the phone and on the computer, opens a few UDP sockets, sends one Mainline `ping` from each to the two bootstrap hosts of the live attempts and prints only port numbers: the local port and the external port that the reply reports, per socket, in order. ### Leaf PORT-1: router port mapping with a fixed, key-ordered set of ports (design only; the owner's decision of 2026-11-18, the details are the engineer's reading and open to correction)

Advances north-star step 4 (the punch) and removes the need for a sweep wherever a router can be asked for a port. **Idea.** The product carries a fixed set of about 100 ports, drawn once uniformly at random from 30000 to 65535 and checked against a list of well-known service ports, so that none is a popular one. Every device that can ask its router for a mapping binds its QUIC socket on a free port from the set and asks the router (UPnP, NAT-PMP, PCP) to forward that same external port to it, so the external endpoint is known in advance and travels in the record and in the signaling. The order in which the set is used is a function of the client's key: the ports sorted by `SHA-256("shaar-port-order/1" || client public key || port)`; the Client uses the first free port in that order, and the Server, which knows the client key from the allowlist and from the signaling, probes the same ports in the same order, so the first candidate is the same on both sides without a message and two clients behind one router do not contend for the same port. **Where it works.** Wherever the router that the device sits behind is the outermost NAT. **Where it does not.** Behind a second NAT that the operator does not control: the phone behind the carrier's NAT (no router protocol reaches it; its external port is random and endpoint-independent, NAT-1) and, as the gateway evidence above shows, this computer, whose router sits behind another NAT. For this pair the set and the order can only serve as the candidate list of a sweep, and the external port of a device behind a random NAT cannot be chosen. **Acceptance of the leaf (proposed).** (1) the set and the order function: a fixed list of 100 distinct ports in 30000 to 65535, none in the list of well-known ports, spread over the range (each ninth of it holds at least five); the order is a permutation of the set, the same for the same key, and differs for different keys; (2) the product binds the first free port of the order and, when the router grants the mapping, publishes the external endpoint; a refused or absent mapping leaves the present behaviour unchanged; (3) in the controlled namespace fixture with a router that grants the mapping, a punch needs no sweep; (4) the live case of this pair is out of reach of this leaf unless the upstream NAT is removed. **The next gateway (a finding while planning step 3, 2026-11-18).** The owner's answer asks for the mapping to go recursively through every router, the outer port taken from the key-ordered list. The first gateway is the default gateway and is known. The gateway beyond it is not told by NAT-PMP, PCP or UPnP (they report only the public address of the router that answers), so the chain needs a way to learn it: a datagram with a time-to-live of two toward a host the products already contact makes the second hop answer with an ICMP error whose source is its address; Linux and Android deliver that error to the socket (`IP_RECVERR`), Windows needs the ICMP helper API, which is FFI (the project's unsafe rule allows it only behind an isolated boundary). ### Design NAT-4: learning the external port of the QUIC socket (design only; no code before the leaf acceptance below)

Advances north-star step 4. **Problem.** The Server's QUIC socket is created and owned by iroh and no DHT node ever sees it; the Mainline socket is another socket with another, random, external port. The record's candidate and the scan centre name the Mainline port, so the Client probes a port that does not belong to QUIC, and NAT-3a showed that a scan around it covers under 1 % of the range. The phone's NAT keeps one external port per socket for every destination (`voted_ports=1`, measured for the Mainline socket), so a port that a DHT node reports for the QUIC socket is the port the Client must probe; the phone must learn that number from the QUIC socket's own traffic.

**Options.** *X, observe through the QUIC socket.* A patch of the pinned iroh copy (a Cargo feature, as the existing `shaar-native-gate`) adds to the endpoint a raw send through the native IP sender and a receive tap in `IpTransport::poll_recv` that recognises a Mainline response from a registered bootstrap address, hands its `ip` field to a channel and neutralises the datagram (first byte zeroed, which iroh already does for non-QUIC packets so that noq ignores them). The Server pings the bootstrap hosts from the QUIC socket at start and every 25 s or so (which also keeps the mapping alive) and publishes the reported port. Keeps iroh's data path (GSO and GRO, multipath); changes how one number is learned; roughly 80 lines of patch plus a fake-host test on the loopback; adds a pinned-source patch with its provenance and feature-off equivalence duties. *Y, a custom UDP transport instead of the IP transport* (`add_custom_transport`; `clear_ip_transports` is already used for the Yggdrasil-only endpoint): the product owns the socket, sends the pings and demultiplexes. iroh treats custom addresses as non-IP paths, so the application-data filter, path selection and the QUIC NAT-traversal candidates, which assume IP, would have to be re-proved; `Endpoint::bound_sockets()` would be empty (the startup reads the address from it); no GSO or GRO unless rebuilt on `noq-udp`. The largest change to the data path. *Z, QUIC over the Mainline socket* (the owner's "one UDP socket shared by the DHT and QUIC"): the owned Mainline socket hands non-KRPC datagrams to a custom transport. The observed Mainline port is then exactly the QUIC port and NAT-2a's answer already carries it; but it has Y's problems and also edits the owned Mainline recipe, and a QUIC short header can start with `d` (0x64), so the split needs a whole-bencode check on every datagram.

**Recommendation: X**, because it changes the least of what is already proved (the data path and the Mainline recipe stay) and it is testable without a NAT. **Verified by reading the pinned copy (2026-11-18).** (1) Every received datagram of every transport passes `Socket::process_datagrams` before noq sees it, and the existing `shaar-native-gate` patch already drops a received datagram there by setting its length to zero (`noq_meta.len = 0; continue`): the tap and its drop use an idiom that is already in the pinned source. The tap must take one datagram at a time (`datagram_count == 1`), because GRO can merge equal-size datagrams of one source. (2) The socket actor already keeps `Vec<Arc<netwatch::UdpSocket>>` of the native sockets (`native_sockets`, used for the close at shutdown) and `netwatch::UdpSocket` has an async `send_to(&[u8], SocketAddr)`, so a raw send needs a public accessor and no change of the send path. (3) The sockets are single-family, not dual-stack: netwatch binds every IP socket with `set_only_v6(true)` ("avoid dualstack") and iroh keeps one IPv4 and one IPv6 socket per endpoint; the IPv6 look of an IPv4 peer (mapped addresses) exists only on the side facing noq. So a raw ping to an IPv4 bootstrap host leaves from the IPv4 socket with a plain IPv4 `SocketAddr`, the accessor must pick the socket by address family (every handle reports its `local_addr`), and the tap sees an IPv4 source in the canonical form. **Still to verify before the leaf:** (4) that the carrier keeps an idle mapping at least 25 s (not measured; QUIC's own keep-alive exists); (5) that nothing changes on the Client, which dials from its own socket. These two readings make X smaller than first sketched: an accessor, one tap in `process_datagrams` and a channel, with no change of `IpTransport` or of the send path. **Acceptance of the leaf (to be fixed with the owner's reading of this note):** on the loopback, a test where a fake bootstrap host reports a port for the endpoint's socket and the Server's record candidate carries it; the feature-off build is byte-identical in behaviour (the existing provenance guard); live, the next pair run records the reported QUIC port next to the Client's probe results. **Order.** NAT-3b first: if the phone's filtering checks address and port, no choice among X, Y and Z reaches the direct path and the limit is recorded with the measurements instead.

### Design KEY-1: create, import and export a key (design only; the owner's goal 3 of 2026-11-18, the details are the engineer's reading)

Advances none of the six end-to-end steps by itself (it is a goal of its own, goal 3 of the north star: a user continues on another device); it touches step 2 (the client is configured with the server key) because the Client's key is the one the Server's allowlist holds. **Today.** A key file is exactly 32 raw bytes (the Ed25519 seed), created with owner-only permissions and never overwritten (`identity/store.rs`); `shaar-client init <server key>` and `shaar-server init` create it, `address` prints the public key, and `address-key new` makes the separate address key. There is no way to carry a key to another device except copying the raw file by hand, and no way to check a copy.

**Proposal.** (1) A portable text form, one line: `shaar-key-1:<64 lowercase hex of the seed>:<8 hex of a checksum>`, the checksum being the first four bytes of `SHA-256("shaar-key-checksum/1" || seed)`, so that a typo or a cut-off copy is refused before anything is written. (2) The library: `Identity::export_text()` returns the line (in a zeroizing buffer) and `Identity::import_text(path, line)` creates the key file from it by the same exclusive, owner-only creation as today (an existing file is never overwritten). (3) The commands, in both binaries because a Server also moves between devices: `key export [--out FILE]` writes the line to a new owner-only file, and prints it to the terminal only with the explicit flag `--print-secret`; `key import [--from FILE]` reads the line from a file or from the standard input, never from an argument (an argument stays in process lists and shell histories), and creates the key file of the place named by `--dir` or `--config`; `key public` prints the public key of the key (the existing `address`, kept). (4) Tests: round trip (export then import gives the same public key), a wrong prefix, a wrong length, non-hex, a bad checksum, an existing file is not overwritten and the error says so, the created file has the owner-only rights (the Unix mode and the Windows DACL checks of the store), import from the standard input, the exported file is owner-only, and nothing prints the seed unless `--print-secret` is given. **Non-goals.** QR codes, a mnemonic phrase, a cloud store, key rotation (accepted as out of the first version on 2026-11-18), the address key (it has its own file and command).

### Design KEY-2: encrypted start (design only; blocked by the owner's reading of KEY-1)

**Proposal.** The key file may hold the seed encrypted. A plain file is exactly 32 bytes; an encrypted one is longer and begins with the magic `SHAARKEY`, so the two never need guessing. Format: magic (8), version (1), the Argon2id parameters (memory in KiB, passes, lanes; 9 bytes), salt (16), nonce (12), then the ChaCha20-Poly1305 sealed seed (32 + 16 bytes) with the header as associated data; defaults 64 MiB, 3 passes, 1 lane, read from the file so that they can be raised later. The loader accepts parameters only inside fixed bounds (at most 256 MiB and 10 passes), so a hostile file cannot make it allocate without limit. **Password sources**, in this order: `--password-file PATH` or the config key `password_file` (an owner-only file, the form for services and for the Android harness, which has no terminal), the standard input when it is not a terminal, and a prompt on the terminal with no echo. With none available the start fails with the typed error `PasswordRequired` and never waits for input in the background. Wrong password and damaged file are both `Decrypt failed`, with no hint which. **Commands:** `key encrypt` (plain file to encrypted, written as a new file and moved over the old one only after it was read back), `key decrypt`, `key password` (change). The password and the derived key are zeroized; the seed stays in memory as it does today. **Dependencies (exact list, quarantine checked 2026-11-19):** `argon2 0.6.0` (published 2026-08-27, with `password-hash 0.6.1`, 2026-04-08), a new package outside the lock; `chacha20poly1305 0.10.1`, `zeroize 1.9.1` and `sha2` are already locked; the terminal prompt would need `rpassword 7.5.5` (published 2026-11-10, past the quarantine), a new package whose own platform calls are not ours to vet, so the prompt can wait and the first version reads the password from a file or the standard input. ### Leaf NAT-6: which of the computer's two NATs picks the random external port (the engineer's design after PORT-3; no new owner decision, inside the standing permission to map through every router that answers)

**Finding DIAG-1 (2026-11-21; why one probe of the walk puts about 100 packets on the wire; code reading only, nothing was built or run; the read-only `rush` run meant for it stalled in its own store maintenance, so the engineer read the code; the vendored sources are under `target/backend/`, paths relative to the repository).** Marks: *read* = seen in the code, *inferred* = follows from read code and a standard QUIC rule, *unknown* = not established.
1. *The probe dials the peer's key, not the address.* `ProbeHandle::probe` builds `EndpointAddr::from_parts(peer, [Ip(address)])` and calls `iroh::Endpoint::connect` under `PROBE_SLICE` of 500 ms (`crates/shaar/src/network/admission/mod.rs:171-178`). The walk starts all probes of a slice at once (`first_to_answer`, `network/signaling/punch/scan.rs:136-163`), up to `WALK_ADDRESSES` (2) hosts times `SCAN_PORTS_PER_ATTEMPT` (20) addresses of the same peer key, next to the offered candidates probed one after another (*read*).
2. *All those connects share one mapped address and one remote state.* `connect_with_opts` resolves the endpoint address to ONE mapped address per peer key and hands only that to the QUIC layer (`iroh/src/endpoint.rs:1177-1199`); `RemoteStateActor::handle_msg_resolve_remote` inserts every address given by a connect into the peer's single path set (`iroh/src/socket/remote_map/remote_state.rs:877-884`) (*read*).
3. *Before a path is selected, every datagram of every connection is sent to every address of the set.* A transmit to the per-peer mapped address becomes `RemoteStateMessage::SendDatagram` (`iroh/src/socket/transports.rs:1215`); with no selected path the actor takes `self.paths.addrs()` as targets (`remote_state.rs:812-852`) and `send_datagram_to_targets` sends the copy to each (`remote_state.rs:1164-1182`) (*read*). A silent probe never selects a path, so each of its datagrams is replicated to the whole set.
4. *The set is not pruned for these addresses.* Pruning starts at 30 non-relay paths and keeps paths "open or of unknown status" (`iroh/src/socket/remote_map/remote_state/path_state.rs:237-312`); the actor ends only after 60 s with no connection (`remote_state.rs:74`, `347-351`) (*read*). While probes of later bursts keep connections coming, the set keeps the addresses of earlier walks (*inferred*; whether the actor idles between the walks of attempt 24 is *unknown*).
5. *How many datagrams.* With `C` simultaneous probes and a set of `A` addresses, one transmission round is between `C(C+1)/2` and `C*A` datagrams (the set grows while the connects register: *inferred*), about 1.2 KB each (the comment at `scan.rs:33`, *read*). A client Initial is not retransmitted inside 500 ms: the initial RTT is 333 ms (`noq-proto/src/config/transport.rs:579`), so the first probe timeout is near 1 s (*inferred* from `pto_base`, `noq-proto/src/connection/paths.rs:820-822`); the dropped connection is closed implicitly (`noq/src/connection.rs:1466-1479`, `1987`) and that close may add one more round (*unknown*: whether a close is sent before the handshake). For `C = A = 40` (two public hosts) one round is 820 to 1600 datagrams, 1.0 to 1.9 MB, for `C = A = 20` (one host) 0.25 to 0.5 MB. Attempt 24 measured 1.8 to 2.6 MB per walk, which fits the two-host case with one to two rounds. The measured "about 100 packets per probe" is `A` (20 to 42) times 2.4 to 5 rounds; the code read does not settle that remaining factor.
6. *Verdict: a default of the library meeting our use of it, not a bug of one line.* The library sends the first flight to every address it knows for a key because a normal caller gives it several addresses of one peer; our walk gives it 40 addresses of one peer in 40 separate connects, so the cost is quadratic in the number of simultaneous probes, and the cost per covered port grows with the size of the slice. The BURST-1 bound limits how often a walk runs, not what one walk costs, and burst 0 (candidates only) also pays `A` datagrams per candidate while the set is alive.
7. *Cheapest bounded change, proposed and not made (leaf BURST-2).* `WALK_ADDRESSES` 2 to 1, `SCAN_PORTS_PER_ATTEMPT` 20 to 10 and `SCAN_RINGS` 5 to 10 (the compile-time check `SCAN_PORTS_PER_ATTEMPT * SCAN_RINGS == ports::PORT_COUNT` in `scan.rs:32` still holds; `WALKS_PER_RUN` follows `SCAN_RINGS`).
